# Method

## The policy

The Conservative pledge (announced 4 October 2026, "a future Conservative government", with no start date) removes the £100,000 income limit on:

- the **30 funded hours for working parents** in England, children aged 9 months to 4 years. Today each parent (and partner) must not expect adjusted net income to exceed £100,000 in the tax year: Childcare (Free of Charge for Working Parents) (England) Regulations 2022 (SI 2022/1134), [reg 14(3)(c)(i)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2022/1134/regulation/14) for the parent and [reg 15(3)(b)(i)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2022/1134/regulation/15) for the partner;
- **Tax-Free Childcare** (UK-wide): the same test for the person and their partner, Childcare Payments (Eligibility) Regulations 2015 (SI 2015/448), [reg 15(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/448/regulation/15), with adjusted net income as in Income Tax Act 2007 s58.

The universal 15 hours for 3- and 4-year-olds has no income test and is unchanged.

## Model

- **policyengine-uk 2.147.2** and policyengine-core 3.33.3, the versions the release manifest pins exactly, are pinned in `pyproject.toml`; the engine refuses any other policyengine-uk. 2.147.2 includes [policyengine-uk#2570](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-uk/pull/2570) (merged 22 October 2026): funded hours end when a child starts school and the working-parent hours start in the term after a child turns 9 months, both from the date of birth.
- **policyengine.py 6.10.0** builds the simulations: the population runs through `managed_microsimulation()` and the cliff example through `calculate_household()`. Its bundle carries policyengine-uk 2.132.6 and does not register this release, so policyengine.py is installed without its `[uk]` extra and the population runs pass the verified file with `allow_unmanaged=True`. `meta` records the installed model version and the bundle's (`policyengine_bundle_model_version`). When a policyengine.py release bundles this release, the runs switch to the managed dataset.

## Weighting and outputs

- Every weighted total, mean, share and decile uses **microdf**: simulation outputs stay as weighted `MicroSeries` / `MicroDataFrame` objects, and person-to-family and family-to-household mappings use the model's own entity mapping (`map_to`). No weights are applied by hand.
- Record-level outputs stay in the gitignored `.cache/runs`. Each cached run carries a provenance key (dataset revision and sha256, policyengine.py and policyengine-uk versions, and a hash of this repository's engine and scenario code); a cached run whose key does not match the current one is refused, never reused.
- Only weighted aggregates reach `data/results.json`. A breakdown cell resting on fewer than ten gaining records, or fewer than ten records whose value changes, is suppressed; if that leaves one suppressed cell in a breakdown, a second cell is suppressed with it, and if no second cell can be suppressed the whole breakdown is withheld. Suppressed cells are published as `null` with `"suppressed": true`, never as zero.
- Nothing is defaulted: a missing provenance field, an unknown git revision or an empty cell stops the build or is published as suppressed.

## The reform in the model

Two parameters are set to infinity for each fiscal year 2026-27 to 2029-30 and nothing else changes (a test walks the whole parameter tree to check):

| Parameter | How the model applies it |
|---|---|
| `gov.dfe.extended_childcare_entitlement.income.limit` | `extended_childcare_entitlement_meets_income_requirements`: the claimant and partner must each have adjusted net income `<= limit` and quarterly earnings of at least 16 hours at the minimum wage, unless one is on a specified benefit and the other meets those conditions (reg 14(4)/15(4)). The family must be in England and meet the work condition. |
| `gov.hmrc.tax_free_childcare.income.income_limit` | `tax_free_childcare_meets_income_requirements`: the claimant and partner must each have `adjusted_net_income <= limit` (and expected earnings over 13 weeks above 16 hours at the minimum wage). |

Costs are the change in weighted spending:

- **30 hours** = change in `extended_childcare_entitlement` + `universal_childcare_entitlement` + `targeted_childcare_entitlement`. policyengine-uk 2.147.2 keeps the universal and targeted 15 hours when a family becomes eligible for the extended entitlement and counts as extended only each child's funded hours above them (`extended_childcare_entitlement_per_child`). The universal and targeted changes are therefore zero on this release, and all three are summed so nothing is missed.
- **Tax-Free Childcare** = change in `tax_free_childcare` (20% of the family's routed childcare spending, capped at £2,000 a child, £4,000 if disabled). `childcare_expenses` is a fixed survey input, so without an adjustment a newly eligible child would get the new funded hours and Tax-Free Childcare on the whole of the care they replace. In each reform run (`engine.net_new_hours`), each child's spending is therefore reduced by the value of its new funded hours (reform less baseline, at the funding rate, floored at zero) before Tax-Free Childcare is computed, as the Microcosm team proposed ([#8](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/pull/8)). Fees are above funding rates, so this removes less than the hours replace. It takes the leg from £0.193bn to £0.144bn in 2027-28 and from £0.214bn to £0.159bn in 2029-30 (`budget.tax_free_childcare_bn`).
- **Take-up (the central figures).** Nobody over £100,000 is eligible today, so the data cannot identify how many newly eligible families would take up the 30 hours. The dataset draws `would_claim_extended_childcare` once per family at the take-up contract's working-parent rate, 0.8361 ([`take_up_contract.json` at microcosm 829c1bc26](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/microcosm/blob/829c1bc26/packages/microcosm-build/src/microcosm/build/uk/take_up_contract.json), the commit the release was cut from), nests `would_claim_universal_childcare` under it at a conditional rate of zero, so the two flags are equal on every record, and draws for each child aged 0 whether it uses the hours (`max_free_entitlement_hours_used`, 0 or 30). A single draw then decides whether each of the few heavily weighted newly eligible families claims. The central cost averages over take-up instead ([#7](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/issues/7)), child by child at DfE's take-up for the child's age (Take-up, below): a claim-and-use pair (`expectation.py`: both claim flags true for every family and `max_free_entitlement_hours_used` 30 for every child, baseline and reform) gives each newly entitled child's 30-hours cost if it takes up the hours, and the central 30 hours is each child's cost there times its rate. Families eligible today are eligible in both runs of the pair, so its change is the newly eligible families'; its universal and targeted lines stay at zero. Each child's Tax-Free Childcare is its netted top-up with the same chance and its top-up before netting otherwise. The family's weekly hours cap (`maximum_extended_childcare_hours_usage`) and the Tax-Free Childcare draws are held as drawn. The pairs are kept out of `config.SCENARIOS`, so adding them leaves every other cached run valid. `budget.take_up` gives the costs on all three take-up bases and `budget.single_draw_bn` on the dataset's single draw; the breakdowns, the range's adjustments and the labour supply offsets are measured on the single draw.
- **Net** = minus the change in `gov_balance`. It equals gross (unrounded, to the pound, in every year): no other tax or benefit in the model depends on these limits. Rounded to £m, the published net and gross figures can differ by £1m.

## Years

Each fiscal year 2026-27 to 2029-30 is costed in full, because the proposal gives no start date. 2026-27 is already half over, so the dashboard leads with **2027-28**, the first full year a change could apply; 2026-27 stays in the tables as illustrative.

## Where the model and the law differ

| # | Issue | Direction for the cost | Handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hours used.** Extended hours are capped by `maximum_extended_childcare_hours_usage`, an input the data draw (0-30 a week; about 28 on average since microcosm#1126); universal hours use `max_free_entitlement_hours_used`, which defaults to 30. policyengine-uk 2.147.2 keeps a 3- or 4-year-old's universal hours when the family becomes eligible and counts only the hours above them as extended, so no family loses (`recipients.families_losing` is 0). | Understates | High end: usage set to the full 30 hours in baseline and reform (`full_30_hour_usage`). |
| 2 | **Under-1s.** A child is eligible from the first term (1 September, 1 January or 1 April) after it turns 9 months. | None | In the model since policyengine-uk#2570: `extended_childcare_entitlement_term_share` counts the year's terms that start after that day, from the date of birth (age and `months_since_last_birthday`, which the release holds for every child). Nothing is added outside the model. |
| 3 | **Pension contributions in adjusted net income.** Income Tax Act 2007 s58 deducts them. | None | Upstream since policyengine-uk#2440: `adjusted_net_income` deducts `pension_contributions_relief`. Salary sacrifice above the April 2029 National Insurance cap no longer raises it (policyengine-uk#2462). |
| 4 | **Strict inequality for the 30 hours.** Earlier policyengine-uk versions tested `ANI < £100,000`; the 2022 Regulations exclude a parent only if ANI is expected to *exceed* £100,000. | None | Fixed upstream: 2.147.2 tests `<=`. |
| 5 | **Tax-Free Childcare routed share.** The release routes 59.3% of childcare spending through a Tax-Free Childcare account (`tax_free_childcare_spend_routed_share` = 0.593 for every record), and the central run uses it. (Builds before the certified release of 4 October 2026 left the model's default of 1, all spending routed.) | Overstates slightly, if fewer families route spending | Low end (`tfc_routed_share`): 0.58 in baseline and reform. |
| 6 | **Childcare spending and the new hours.** Spending is a survey input. | Overstates slightly | Each reform run nets the value of a child's new funded hours off its spending before Tax-Free Childcare (Costs, above); fees above funding rates leave a little too much spending. The hours response recomputes cash income and the Tax-Free Childcare price on the care still bought, on its own displacement rule (Behaviour). |
| 7 | **A partner on a specified benefit.** SI 2022/1134 reg 14(4) and 15(4) exempt a partner with limited capability for work or a specified benefit from the work and income conditions when the other parent meets them; SI 2015/448 reg 13 regards such a partner as in qualifying paid work for Tax-Free Childcare. | None for the benefits the data hold | Upstream in 2.147.2 (`extended_childcare_entitlement_limited_capability_or_specified_benefit`, `tax_free_childcare_regarded_as_in_paid_work`). Not covered: limited capability for work itself, NI credits and carer's leave, which the data do not hold. |
| 8 | **Reception-age 4-year-olds.** In law the early-years entitlement ends when a child starts school, the September after its fourth birthday in England. | None | In the model since policyengine-uk#2570: `early_years_term_share_before_school` keeps only the terms that start before then, from the date of birth (not the data's `current_education`). Most 4-year-olds keep the summer term. |
| 9 | **Expected, not realised, income.** The law tests the adjusted net income a parent *expects* for the tax year when applying or reconfirming (SI 2022/1134 reg 14(3)(c)(i) and 15(3)(b)(i); SI 2015/448 reg 15(1)); the model tests realised annual income. A parent who expected to stay under £100,000 but ended the year above it can already receive support, which the model counts as a reform cost; a parent who expected to exceed £100,000 but ended below it is modelled as eligible today. CenTax find that a third (33%) of parents whose year-end income was £100,000-£120,000 claimed and received some free childcare ([Removing the childcare cliff-edge](https://centax.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AdvaniFlewPepin-HallSummers2026_Removing-the-childcare-cliff-edge.pdf), September 2026, pp. 5-6 and 59-60). | Unknown | Reported; not in the range. The first effect lowers the true cost, the second raises it, and the data cannot separate them. |
| 10 | **Tax-Free Childcare alongside Universal Credit.** In law a family cannot use Tax-Free Childcare while it claims Universal Credit; the model does not make the two mutually exclusive. | Baseline only (families gaining from the reform are above £100,000 and not on UC) | Reported; it raises the modelled baseline TFC spend. |
| 11 | **Funded-hours rates.** | None | Upstream since policyengine-uk#2465: `gov.dfe.childcare_funding_rate` holds DfE's 2025-26 and 2026-27 national averages (£12.04, £8.90 and £6.61 an hour in 2026-27), uprated by CPI after that. |

## The range

The low and high ends add the sensitivities above to the central estimate: the high end full 30-hour usage, the low end 58% of spending routed through Tax-Free Childcare. Every pair nets the new hours off spending in its reform, as the central pair does. The effects are measured on the single draw and added to each take-up basis's total (`budget.take_up.bases[id].range_bn`); full usage still applies on DfE's and CenTax's rates, which count whether a child takes a place but not how many hours it uses.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full 30-hour usage | +0.05 | +0.06 | +0.06 | +0.07 |
| TFC routing at 58% | -0.002 | -0.002 | -0.003 | -0.003 |
| **Low** | 0.98 | 1.04 | 1.10 | 1.19 |
| **Central** (DfE's take-up by age) | 0.98 | 1.05 | 1.11 | 1.20 |

**Tax-Free Childcare take-up among newly eligible families** is a separate line, not in the range. The `would_claim_tfc` draw is a claim flag (97% of families with a child under 12), fitted to HMRC's top-up and account counts; a family gets a top-up only on the paid registered childcare it reports, so use among all eligible families is about a third. Nobody over £100,000 is eligible today, so the data cannot identify their rate. The line sets it to 70% of newly eligible families with spending, about today's use among all eligible families (the Microcosm team's analysis on [#8](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/pull/8)), on an expectation basis: the netted leg with every eligible family claiming (`tfc_all` runs) times 0.7. It gives £0.099bn in 2027-28 and £0.111bn in 2029-30, against £0.144bn and £0.159bn centrally.

## Sampling uncertainty

`budget.uncertainty` is a bootstrap over FRS source households (`aggregate.bootstrap`): `household_source_id`, read from the pinned release and joined to the simulated households through their person ids, identifies each FRS household, and each of the 2,000 draws resamples the source households with replacement, each with all its Microcosm copies (SPI, high-value-home and CGT copies), the release weights fixed, for every costed year. It holds the calibration fixed and ignores the FRS's clustering, so it is a floor on the sampling uncertainty, not the full sampling error.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 hours, DfE's take-up by age: SD (5th-95th percentile) | 0.130 (0.640-1.068) | 0.137 (0.686-1.138) | 0.139 (0.728-1.182) | 0.144 (0.811-1.284) |

| 30 hours, CenTax's rates: SD (5th-95th percentile) | 0.128 (0.619-1.041) | 0.135 (0.665-1.113) | 0.137 (0.707-1.150) | 0.142 (0.786-1.257) |

On the single draw it reproduces the Microcosm team's bootstrap on a133f37a ([#8](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/pull/8#issuecomment-6370235552): SD about £0.16bn and £0.17bn, 5th-95th £0.73-1.27bn and £0.85-1.42bn). Averaging over take-up removes part of the spread: on DfE's rates the 30 hours' SD is about £0.03bn smaller than on the single draw, and every basis is resampled with the same draws. On the single draw the 30-hours cost in 2027-28 rests on about 170 FRS households, an effective number of about 40 (Kish, over their shares of the cost); the ten largest gaining families carry 31% of it (`reconciliation.concentration`). CenTax's static £0.80bn and £0.98bn lie inside both years' ranges.

**Age profile (one-sided).** No calibration row crosses income with child age, and no published source independent of the FRS does either. The release has few 1-year-olds in families with a parent over £100,000: 5%, against 9-13% at the other ages under 5 (`budget.age_profile_sensitivity`). A 1-year-old is the dearest age to fund, so this can only understate the cost. Holding the number of newly entitled 1-to-4-year-olds fixed and spreading them evenly across those ages, each at its own mean cost per child, raises the 2027-28 30-hours cost on the single draw by £0.157bn; on CenTax's gradient between under-3s and 3-to-4s the Microcosm team find about £0.13bn. Babies (age 0) are about £0.10bn of that year's cost.

## Model corrections

`src/childcare_100k/corrections.py` replaces one policyengine-uk 2.147.2 formula in every run, baseline and reform alike, and in the household calculator. It is fingerprinted, so a change upstream stops the build instead of being silently overwritten; the engine checks the correction reached each population simulation, and the household calculator checks it reached its own.

- **Tax-Free Childcare's final income gate tests the claimant and partner** (C6, Tax-Free Childcare half): Childcare Payments Act 2014 s.3(1) requires the claimant's partner to meet the income condition (SI 2015/448 reg 15). 2.147.2's `tax_free_childcare_eligible` still applies it to members flagged `is_parent`, so an unflagged partner (a step-parent, say) over £100,000 would pass; the replacement uses upstream's `is_claimant_or_partner`.

Checked against the 2.147.2 source and removed as upstream: salary sacrifice above the April 2029 National Insurance cap (C4; policyengine-uk#2462: the cap now changes only National Insurance earnings, and `salary_sacrifice_returned_to_income` has no formula), and the three toggles of the line on CenTax's basis, pension contributions in adjusted net income (#2440), DfE's funding rates (#2465) and reception (#2570), with the 9-11-month-olds added outside the model (#2570). Five corrections from earlier versions were removed before that because policyengine-uk now does the same (checked against its source, and kept as regression tests in `tests/test_corrections.py`): exactly £100,000 qualifies for the 30 hours (C3: `ani <= p.income.limit`); a partner on a specified benefit need not meet the work and income conditions (C1: `extended_childcare_entitlement_limited_capability_or_specified_benefit` in the work condition); the universal and targeted hours are kept and the extended value is the hours above them (C2: `universal_childcare_entitlement_eligible`, `targeted_childcare_entitlement_eligible`, `extended_childcare_entitlement_per_child`); Tax-Free Childcare regards a partner on a caring or incapacity benefit as in paid work (C5: `tax_free_childcare_regarded_as_in_paid_work`); and the 30 hours' and Tax-Free Childcare's work conditions test the claimant and partner (the rest of C6: `is_claimant_or_partner`).

## Behaviour

The headline costs are **static**: nobody changes how much they work. A labour supply response is costed alongside them (`labour_supply` in the results; `src/childcare_100k/labour_supply.py` and `hours_response.py`, ported from [PolicyEngine/free-childcare-reform](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/free-childcare-reform)) and gives a **dynamic cost** = static total − the money it brings back. The dynamic cost is an illustrative scenario, not a forecast: the hours price elasticity is extrapolated, neither the OBR elasticities nor Brewer's estimate is measured on parents over £100,000, and the range is not a confidence interval. It runs as its own cached job (`labour_supply`) on the central baseline and reform simulations, with the same provenance key as every other run, because the gain to work needs live simulations.

**Who responds.** Every adult (the first two in each family; not self-employed, students or aged 60 and over, the exclusions of both OBR models, Tables A3 and A4) in a family whose **youngest child is under 12** and in which **at least one adult's income, as the limits test it** (adjusted net income, after pension contributions), **is over £100,000** in the baseline: the parent over the limit as well as their partner. Under 12 because Tax-Free Childcare runs to 11 and contains the 30 hours' 9 months-4 years band; following Brewer et al., it is the parent whose *youngest* child is in the band that the support frees to work. Over £100,000 because that is the only place the reform changes anything. Those not in work may move into work (in practice the partner of a parent over the limit, who today gains no childcare support by working and under the reform brings the family the 30 hours and Tax-Free Childcare by doing so); those in work may change their hours. Disabled adults (the model's `is_disabled_for_benefits`, receipt of DLA or PIP) cannot move into work, because the OBR's participation model excludes them (Table A4), but keep the hours response, because its progression model does not (Table A3). The job checks that the reform leaves everyone else's gain to work unchanged. On this release it does not for about 1,300 adults in 2026-27 and 2027-28, two survey records (non-working adults whose own income is just under £100,000, so entering work would take them over the limit). They are not given a response, and `test_labour_supply_block` fails on it until the population definition is settled ([#11](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/issues/11)); the build allows up to 0.2% of the responding adults outside the population and publishes the count (`adults_moved_outside_population`). About 0.9-1.1 million adults are in the participation population.

**Moving into work (extensive margin).** OBR participation elasticities (Table A1: by sex, partner's work, age of youngest child and earnings quintile; policyengine-uk's `calculate_participation_elasticities`). Each is the percentage change in the probability of working for a percentage change in income in work; Adam and Phillips (IFS R75, Appendix E) convert it to the gain to work G by G / I, where I is in-work income (with G = I − O this is the familiar 1 − replacement rate). Our gain to work nets off childcare, so the factor is the childcare-adjusted G / I actually used. Because the elasticity is a change in the *probability* of working, it applies to the employed share: for a group with employment rate P the employed share rises by P × e × dG/G. As in Adam and Phillips, who reweight the working records, new employment is the weighted sum over responding *workers* of their own e × dG/G; those entrants are given the earnings and fiscal profile of responding non-workers *like the workers who imply them*: the non-workers in the same OBR cell (sex, in a couple or not, the youngest child's age band 0-2, 3-5, 6-10 or 11, and earnings quintile, the groups of Table A1), shared in proportion to each one's own e × dG/G. Where a cell has no responding non-worker, its entrants go to the nearest coarser cell (dropping the quintile, then the child's age band, then everyone); `allocated_by_cell_level` reports how many land at each level. No non-worker's probability exceeds 1; entry above that is dropped and reported (`entry_capped`). A worker whose gain to work falls leaves with probability e × |dG/G|. The gain to work is in-work less out-of-work household net income, each recomputed by the model, **net of the childcare a parent pays to work**: a non-worker is given the mean childcare spend of working parents whose youngest child is the same age, pro-rated to 18.8 hours a week, less the Tax-Free Childcare the scenario would pay on it (from the model's own in-work eligibility); the 30 hours reach them through the model. Entrants work 18.8 hours at the median hourly wage of workers of their sex and age band (policyengine-uk's `impute_wages_for_nonworkers`). Responses are expected values, not random draws. The Exchequer gains an entrant's earnings less the rise in their household's net income (tax and National Insurance paid, less the childcare support the family now receives) and less the Tax-Free Childcare top-up on their new childcare.

**Hours (intensive margin).** Every responding adult in work, at or below £100,000 and over it (published separately), in two parts. The combined earnings change of every responding adult, both parts, is put back into the reform simulation once, which recomputes tax and benefits; the offset is the extra earnings less the rise in household net income. Taxes are not additive in the parts: a parent at £99,900 whose price effect adds £839 and income effect removes £160 crosses the personal-allowance taper, and recomputing the parts separately and adding them overstated that parent's offset by £32 (£432.87 against £400.84, with the income effect on the government-cost basis below). The parts are therefore an attribution that adds up to the net exactly: the adults at or below £100,000 recomputed on their own and those over it as the remainder; within each group, the price change recomputed on its own and the income effect as the remainder. Hours and earnings move together at a constant hourly wage.

- *Price effect* (`hours_response.py`): an assumed childcare-price elasticity of hours of −0.042 times the change in the price of the family's **marginal** hour of paid childcare, for adults whose family pays for childcare. The elasticity is an extrapolated scenario assumption, not an estimated price elasticity: Brewer, Cattan, Crawford and Rabe (IFS WP20/09, Table A.3 panel B) estimate +0.600 weekly hours, on a mean of 14.319 including zeros for non-workers, for mothers whose youngest child becomes eligible for full-time instead of part-time free care. Treating that eligibility as a 100% price fall extends it to other interventions, to fathers, to child ages and to incomes it was not estimated on; it is a total-hours estimate, so it overlaps with the extensive margin. **What counts as a price change**: only a change in what an extra hour of care costs. The marginal net price of the next £1 of paid care is £1 less all the cash support the model ties to `childcare_expenses` on it (Tax-Free Childcare, the Universal Credit childcare element, and the legacy tax-credit, Housing Benefit and Childcare Grant links): the model's own rate, the change in that support when the family buys £1 more care (split across its children in proportion to their spend, so near a cap it is the rate on the next pound, not an average over spending beyond it), today and, under the reform, at the spend left once the funded hours displace paid care, the income effect's spending. Tax-Free Childcare pays 20% of each routed pound until the child's cap (with the 59.3% routed share, about 12p per £1), so where the reform newly pays it below the cap it lowers the price (a family at the £2,000 cap on £11,000 of care faces 20% on the £7,710.38 left); the UC childcare element pays 85p per £1 up to its cap (SI 2013/376 regs 34 and 36), so where displacement takes a UC family below the cap its next £1 falls from £1 to 15p (María's A8 household: £27,000 of care, element capped at £22,033.92, £20,153.82 on the £23,710.38 left). Sensitivities (`intensive_price_basis`): the reform's rate at today's spend (`original_spend`, hours offset £0.167bn in 2029-30) and Tax-Free Childcare alone (`tfc_only`, the rule before A8, £0.179bn) against £0.179bn central. The 30 funded hours are a fixed amount, conditional only on both parents meeting the minimum earnings test, so for a family that still buys paid care on top of them they do not change what an extra hour costs: they are inframarginal and enter only through the income effect. Only where they are worth more than all the paid care the family buys does the marginal hour become free (a 100% fall, Brewer's setting); funded hours worth exactly the spend cover the hours bought, and the next hour is still paid for. Spending is recorded in pounds, not hours, so coverage is judged on value: newly funded hours are *assumed* to displace paid care at 90.5% of their value (IFS BN189, pp. 11-12: 570 more funded hours raised subsidisable care by 163 hours and all non-family care by 54; 1 − 54/570 counts every displaced hour of non-family care as paid; 1 − 163/570 = 71.4% is the lower end, 100% the upper). The rule is the same for both groups, since a family buys one set of childcare.
- *Income effect*: the OBR income elasticities (Table A2; policyengine-uk's `calculate_labour_net_income_elasticities`: −0.185 for a woman in a couple whose youngest child is 0-2, down to −0.037 for a lone mother of a 0-4-year-old; −0.05 for a man in a couple), applied as policyengine-uk's `apply_progression_responses` does: earnings × elasticity × the household's gain from the reform, before any response, as a percentage of its disposable income (`hbai_household_net_income`; clipped to ±100%). Every responding adult in work. A family made better off works slightly less, so this part costs money. The OBR elasticities are responses to cash income, and the funded hours are not cash: what they put in a family's pocket is the paid childcare they replace. So the gain is measured on the same disposable-income basis as its denominator: the change in `hbai_household_net_income` (cash taxes and benefits, Tax-Free Childcare included, funded hours not) plus the paid care the newly funded hours displace (their funding value at the 90.5% displacement assumption below, capped at what the family pays for care, summed over the household's families). The reform's cash income is recomputed with each child's spend lowered by the family's displaced share, so the Tax-Free Childcare top-up and Universal Credit childcare element on the care it stops buying go together (a London UC family on £100,001 and £16,000 loses £2,796.18 of childcare element; in the population this moves the offset by under £100). For a 2027-28 couple on £120,000 and £40,000 with a three-year-old in £5,000 of paid care, the newly funded hours displace £3,289.62 of it; the reform's top-up on the £1,710.38 left is £342.08, not £1,000, so the gain is £3,631.70 rather than £4,289.62. Two sensitivities (`intensive_income_basis`): holding childcare spending fixed (row 6), so that support on displaced care is kept (`paid_care_fixed_spend`), and counting the funded hours at what the government pays for them (`government_cost`, the change in `household_net_income`). The base is disposable income rather than `household_net_income`, which also deducts policyengine-uk's expected stamp duty: one surveyed family (weight about 8,000) with £160,000 of earnings and £106,000 a year of expected stamp duty had a household net income falling from £9,600 in 2026-27 to £1,400 in 2028-29 and below zero in 2029-30, so its percentage change, and the over-£100,000 income effect, jumped in 2028-29 and fell back.

**Couples.** The OBR groups in Tables A1 and A2 ("married or cohabiting" women, lone parents, men other than lone fathers) turn on whether a person has a partner. policyengine-uk 2.147.2 still assigns them with `is_married`, which Microcosm supplies as legal marriage or civil partnership, so a cohabiting father would get no elasticity and a cohabiting mother the lone-parent rates; about a fifth of couples with children are not married. The participation and income elasticities here are assigned to married and cohabiting couples alike, by the model's `is_couple` (`labour_supply.CoupleView`), leaving `is_married` and the tax that depends on it untouched ([policyengine-uk#2190](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-uk/issues/2190)).

The displacement rate also sets how much paid care the funded hours save in the income effect: the `intensive_displacement` sensitivity moves the 2029-30 hours offset from £0.179bn to £0.138bn at 71.4% and £0.186bn at 100%. `tfc_only` matches the central figure to the nearest £1m, so on this release the Universal Credit childcare element barely moves any paying adult's marginal price. In the income effect (`intensive_income_basis`), keeping the support on displaced care makes it −£0.056bn rather than −£0.053bn and the hours offset £0.176bn; counting the funded hours at government cost makes it −£0.091bn and the hours offset £0.142bn. (The record counts behind these, such as how many paying adults the funded hours fully cover, were last measured on the 2 October release and are not restated.)

**Range.** Low and high scale every elasticity (participation, price and income) by 1/3 and 2. The range is illustrative, not a sourced uncertainty interval: the factors are the ratios of childcare-price elasticities of maternal *employment* of −0.05 and −0.30 to a central −0.15 (a reading of Akgündüz and Plantenga's meta-analysis for the UK, whose Table 1 gives UK responses of −0.09 for single and −0.138 for married mothers), a different outcome from the OBR participation and income elasticities and the hours price elasticity they scale. The dynamic range below should not be read as a confidence interval.

**Why so few move into work.** In 2029-30 about 1.07 million adults respond. The OBR elasticities with respect to in-work income fall sharply with respect to the gain to work, because the gain to work is small next to the household's in-work income for a partner whose partner earns over £100,000. Applied to the employed share, that gives about 2,200 entrants in 2029-30. About 900 are placed among non-workers in the same OBR cell as the workers who imply them and about 1,300 in the same sex, couple and child-age group without the earnings quintile; none needs a coarser cell. In 2026-27 to 2028-29 the cap of 1 on a non-worker's probability drops about 300 entrants (`entry_capped`); in 2029-30 it drops none. `entry_sensitivity` separates the effect of the coarser-cell entrants, at central elasticities: dropping them leaves about 900 entrants and an offset of −£8.5m (`same_cell_only`), and giving them the weighted median hourly wage of the workers who imply them turns the offset to −£11.0m (`worker_profile`), against −£16.4m centrally. They cost money rather than bring it back: the tax and National Insurance on 18.8 hours is outweighed by the 30 hours and Tax-Free Childcare their family now receives, so the extensive offset is −£0.016bn. A participation response raises the cost here because the 30 hours and Tax-Free Childcare need both parents in work: the partner's entry is what makes the family eligible. The hours response, of parents already in work and already eligible, changes earnings without unlocking support, so it brings money back (a net £0.179bn in 2029-30). There are no leavers.

**Not modelled: bunching.** Parents who today keep their income at or just below £100,000 and would earn more without the limit do not appear in the survey data, so their response is not modelled. CenTax estimate it from HMRC records at +£210m in 2029-30 (free hours only).

**Against CenTax.** CenTax (Table 4.2, 2030 = 2029-30, free hours only) find +£130m from partners entering work and +£210m from parents no longer keeping income below £100,000. Our extensive margin covers both schemes and adds £0.016bn to the cost in 2029-30 rather than bringing money back: CenTax's partner response implies far more entrants than the OBR's elasticities give for second earners in high-income households, and it is not clear that CenTax net off the childcare support entrants' families then receive. Their £210m has no counterpart here, and our hours response has none in theirs. Neither figure is a target.

The household calculator and the breakdowns by income, family type and region are static.

With Tax-Free Childcare the totals are £0.981bn, £1.046bn, £1.107bn and £1.196bn on DfE's rates, £0.961bn, £1.024bn, £1.085bn and £1.172bn on CenTax's, and £1.004bn, £1.071bn, £1.133bn and £1.225bn on the flat rate.

**What the child-level rates count, and the pair they apply to.** DfE's figures are registered children over DfE's estimate of eligible children (from the FRS, the SPI and the censuses), so they count both whether a family applies and whether the child takes a place. They therefore replace the dataset's claim draw and its under-2 usage draw rather than adding to them: they apply to the claim-and-use pair (`baseline_claim_use_all` / `reform_claim_use_all`), in which every family claims and every child uses its hours (`max_free_entitlement_hours_used` 30). How many hours a week a child who takes a place uses is a different question, which DfE's ratio does not answer, so the family's weekly hours cap (`maximum_extended_childcare_hours_usage`, drawn to DfE's census, about 28.5 hours on average) stays as drawn, and the range's full-usage adjustment still applies. The cost is each newly entitled child's change in funded hours in the pair times the rate for its DfE age (`aggregate.person_changes`). For the families and children gaining (`budget.take_up.bases[id].recipients`, expected counts) each child takes up independently of its siblings; on the flat rate the family takes up as a whole.

**DfE's caveats.** DfE says the 2025 percentages, and its estimates of eligible children, should be treated with caution because they rest on survey data, and that it will revise them in next year's publication. Its estimate of eligible 9-11-month-olds does not account for maternity or paternity leave. A substantial share of 2-year-olds eligible for both the working-parent and the disadvantaged 2-year-old entitlements were recorded under the working-parent one, so its count includes some children who should have been recorded under the disadvantaged offer, which may put the 84% too high. And the rates for 9-11-month-olds and 1- and 2-year-olds were measured in January 2025, when the under-3 working-parent offer was 15 hours a week; it rose to 30 hours in September 2025, and take-up of 30 hours may differ. CenTax apply the same January 2025 rates to the 30-hour offer, so the comparison with them holds. For a low case, CenTax's low-cost scenario assumes 60% take-up, the share of parents below £100,000 who held a live childcare account in 2022 (pp. 100-101).

**Age.** DfE's January census counts children by their age on 31 December, and its 9-11-month group is the children funded from the spring term. Each child's rate is read at its DfE age in the model year, the age at the census in that year's spring term: the year less policyengine-uk's `birth_year`. DfE age 0 is 42%, 1 is 70%, 2 is 84% and 3 and 4 are 91%; a child born September to December is funded for the summer term before it starts reception (aged 4 then) and has DfE age 5, so it takes the 3-and-4 rate. CenTax's under-3s are DfE ages 0 to 2. The model's own ages are whole years on 6 October; reading the rates at that age instead would put children born from October to December a year younger, at a lower rate (or, for CenTax, the 2-year-olds born then at 73% rather than 91%), and give slightly lower costs on both child-level bases. The rate a child takes is the one for its age at the year's census; a child who moves up an age band during the year keeps it for the whole year.

**Tax-Free Childcare on the new hours.** Each reform run nets the value of a child's new funded hours off its childcare spending before Tax-Free Childcare is computed (Costs, above), so how much is netted depends on whether the child takes up the hours, and the Tax-Free Childcare leg follows the 30 hours' basis. Tax-Free Childcare is a per-child top-up in policyengine-uk, depending on the child's own spending, so on a child-level basis each child's top-up is its netted top-up with its take-up chance and its top-up before netting otherwise; on the flat rate the family's netted and un-netted top-ups are weighted by the rate, as in #7. The value netted is the hours' value on the basis's own pair: every child using its hours, on the child-level bases. Lower take-up of the hours therefore means a little more Tax-Free Childcare (£0.146bn on CenTax's rates against £0.144bn on DfE's in 2027-28).

**Why DfE's rates are the headline.** They are the official measure, and they are by age. The release itself sits close to them: among children eligible today, about 75% at age 1, 82% at 2 and 90% at 3 receive the working-parent hours at some point in 2027-28 ([review of #12](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/pull/12#issuecomment-6385210332); an annual rather than a January measure, so a rough check), as a build calibrated to DfE's January 2025 registration counts should. On DfE's rates, baseline and reform therefore sit on the same take-up by age. Against them the flat rate is above DfE's at 9-11 months, where on the claim-all pair the dataset's under-2 usage draw makes it about 73% in effect, and at age 1; level at 2; and below at 3 and 4. So it gives £0.93bn in 2027-28 against £0.90bn on DfE's rates, a £27m gap that splits +£18m at DfE age 0, +£35m at 1, −£2m at 2 and −£25m at 3 to 5 (`budget.take_up.contract_gap_by_dfe_age`).

**Against CenTax.** CenTax's 73% is DfE's 2025 take-up for all 9-month- to 2-year-olds together. On the same runs it gives £0.88bn in 2027-28 and £1.01bn in 2029-30, about £0.02-0.03bn below DfE's rates by age, because among the newly entitled under-3s the cost is mostly for 2-year-olds, whom DfE's rates put at 84% rather than 73%, and little for the 9-11-month-olds at 42%. CenTax's own static costs (£0.80bn and £0.98bn) are lower still; the rest of the gap is in who is counted and their incomes (The £0.7bn benchmark, below).

**The single draw.** On the dataset's own draw the newly eligible families drew a claim more often than the contract's rate (88% weighted, against 83% unweighted), so it is the highest of the four. It is kept as `budget.single_draw_bn`, and the breakdowns by family, income, region and age, the range's adjustments, the concentration figures and the labour supply offsets are measured on it.

**Tax-Free Childcare.** The `would_claim_tfc` draw is held fixed: it is a claim flag, and use is limited by reported paid childcare. `assumptions` in the results gives each draw's rate overall and where a parent is over £100,000, the share of families reporting paid registered childcare, and the share receiving a top-up. The Tax-Free Childcare take-up alternative is in The range, above.

## Baseline validation

`baseline_validation` sets the unreformed model against HMRC's income tax liabilities statistics (people with income of £100,000 or more, July 2026 Table 2.5), HMRC's Tax-Free Childcare statistics (June 2026), DfE's January 2025 early years census (the census the release's DfE rows bind; working-parent children are counted as those rows count them, funded in the January term by their age on 31 December) and DfE's early years funding technical notes for 2025-26 and 2026-27 (free-entitlements funding: each total is the sum of the note's illustrative totals by age, since neither note states a combined figure). The figures and what they show are in the README's validation table and `baseline_validation`.

## The £0.7bn benchmark

The Conservatives put the cost at about £700m a year when they announced the pledge (PA: "The Conservatives estimate the policy would cost £700 million a year"; BBC), funded by cutting staff at arm's-length public bodies (about £1.6bn by the end of the decade). The party's web announcement covers both the free hours and Tax-Free Childcare ("abolish the £100,000 childcare cliff edge for both free childcare hours and Tax-Free Childcare") and gives no figure or method. City AM says the costing "is based on a recent report by the Centre for the Analysis of Taxation" and that removing the Tax-Free Childcare limit as well "pushed up the costs slightly". CenTax label tax years by their later year (report p.2), so their "2030" is 2029-30, the year we compare. CenTax's report ([*Removing the childcare cliff-edge: impacts and cost of reform*](https://centax.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AdvaniFlewPepin-HallSummers2026_Removing-the-childcare-cliff-edge.pdf), September 2026, Table 4.2) covers the **free childcare hours only**: a **static** cost of £980m in 2030, and a **net** cost of £640m after £340m of extra revenue: £210m of tax from parents who no longer keep their income below £100,000 (the intensive margin) and £130m of tax and National Insurance from partners who enter work (the extensive margin; Table 4.2 and the sentence after it). It does not cover Tax-Free Childcare.

The like-for-like comparison is therefore **our 30-hours leg against CenTax's static figures** (both static, both the free hours only): £800m in 2028 (2027-28) and £980m in 2030 (2029-30) after their deduction for parents bunching just under £100,000, £860m and £1,070m before it, or £520m and £640m net. Our 30-hours leg is £0.902bn in 2027-28 and £1.037bn in 2029-30, 13% and 6% above CenTax's static figures, and 5% above and 3% below them before bunching; on CenTax's own take-up rates it is £0.878bn and £1.011bn, 10% and 3% above (Take-up, above). Both CenTax figures lie inside our bootstrap ranges (Sampling uncertainty, above): our cost rests on about 170 FRS households in 2027-28, against CenTax's HMRC records for the whole population. Microcosm has about 284,000 adults over £100,000 (after pension contributions) with a child under 5 in 2027-28 (`reconciliation.parents_over_100k_with_child_under_5_thousands`), against CenTax's roughly 200,000 parents of a child aged 9 months to 4; by the Microcosm team's reading (#8), CenTax can only count parents they find in HMRC records, which misses many single-earner couples, and those mostly fail the partner work test. Take-up (CenTax's two rates against DfE's by age), the income distribution above £100,000, and expected against realised income also differ. The £0.7bn is close to CenTax's net free-hours figure, £0.64bn, plus a small Tax-Free Childcare addition. Our total covers both schemes on the same static basis as our 30-hours leg.

**Against the previous release.** On a858c100 with policyengine-uk 2.134.4, the 30-hours leg on CenTax's basis (the reception, DfE-rate and pension toggles, with 9-11-month-olds added outside the model) was £0.872bn in 2027-28 and £0.961bn in 2029-30, on the single take-up draw. On the same basis it is £0.112bn and £0.158bn higher here. By the child's age (whole years on 6 October), single draw, £bn:

- **Age 4.** The old reception toggle removed the whole year from every 4-year-old the data record in reception. policyengine-uk 2.147.2 keeps the terms before a child starts school, as the law does, so most 4-year-olds keep the summer term. Funding rates are the same in both runs (£12.32, £9.10 and £6.76 an hour in 2027-28).
- **Age 0.** The old add-on funded each age-0 child for 1.125 months; the engine's term rule, with ages from the date of birth, funds the age-0 cohort for about 2.7 months and the age-1 cohort for about 11 rather than 12. Over a child's life the two come to about the same. But among families with a parent over £100,000 the sample holds about 73,000 children aged 0 against 33,000 aged 1, so the months moved from age 1 to age 0 are weighted more than twice as heavily. The DfE row for 9-11-month-olds in the January term is matched (29,100 against 29,155), so the imbalance is within the families over £100,000, not in the overall count; it is a question for the data (PR #8).
- **Age 2.** 2-year-olds on the working-parent hours are 6% above DfE's January 2025 count in the baseline (README).
- Hours used (28.5 a week among newly eligible families) are not a driver; the take-up draw's luck is (Take-up, above).

## Cliff example

One illustrative family, computed with policyengine.py's `calculate_household()` for 2027-28: a couple in the South East with children aged 2 and 3, £20,000 a year of their own childcare spending, one parent on £40,000. Household net income after that spending is £102,205 when the other parent earns £99,000, £102,785 at exactly £100,000 (which still qualifies, as in law), and £84,552 at £100,001; with the limits removed it is £102,786 at £100,001, a cliff of about £18,200.
