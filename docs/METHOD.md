# Method

## The policy

The Conservative pledge (announced 4 October 2026, "a future Conservative government", with no start date) removes the £100,000 income limit on:

- the **30 funded hours for working parents** in England, children aged 9 months to 4 years. Today each parent (and partner) must not expect adjusted net income to exceed £100,000 in the tax year: Childcare (Free of Charge for Working Parents) (England) Regulations 2022 (SI 2022/1134), [reg 14(3)(c)(i)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2022/1134/regulation/14) for the parent and [reg 15(3)(b)(i)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2022/1134/regulation/15) for the partner;
- **Tax-Free Childcare** (UK-wide): the same test for the person and their partner, Childcare Payments (Eligibility) Regulations 2015 (SI 2015/448), [reg 15(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/448/regulation/15), with adjusted net income as in Income Tax Act 2007 s58.

The universal 15 hours for 3- and 4-year-olds has no income test and is unchanged.

## Data

- **Microcosm UK 2024-25, the national release cut on 21 October 2026,** is the only dataset: the file `microcosm_uk_2024_25.h5` in `policyengine/populace-uk-private` at the release's immutable cut tag `microcosm-uk-2024-25-national-20261021T195620Z-a858c100`, commit `415b034b2a46ba536065c48754636e916ed096a1` (as the Hugging Face download records it), sha256 `e1f027b98a5127329643abc62e2de47b8fd67344b311ab1d9b86c7411908d190`.
- **What the release changed** ([PolicyEngine/microcosm#1338](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/microcosm/issues/1338), tracked in #1339), against the 19 October release (6cba5d71) used before: universal take-up is nested under working-parent take-up (before, the two were drawn independently, so removing the limit appeared to fund some 3- and 4-year-olds' universal hours as well); 4-year-olds in reception are out of the take-up fit and the DfE calibration rows; SPI and support copies draw their own childcare take-up and hours; and ONS single-year-of-age and DfE registration targets are bound. The release carries its own signed certification. It inherits [microcosm#1126](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/microcosm/issues/1126): `maximum_extended_childcare_hours_usage` is drawn to DfE's January 2025 census, a mean of about 28.5 hours a week.
- **This repository pins and verifies the file itself**, as PolicyEngine/uk-energy-reforms does: it downloads the file at that commit from Hugging Face (`HF_TOKEN` or `HUGGING_FACE_TOKEN`), or with `HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1` reads it from the local Hugging Face cache without a token, checks the sha256, and stops on any mismatch. There is no fallback dataset.
- The Enhanced FRS and the earlier `populace_uk_2023` build are not used.

## Model

- **policyengine-uk 2.134.4**, the version the release manifest pins exactly (2.102.3 silently drops stored inputs from this file), is pinned in `pyproject.toml`; the engine refuses any other version.
- **policyengine.py 6.10.0** builds the simulations: the population runs through `managed_microsimulation()` and the cliff example through `calculate_household()`. Its bundle carries policyengine-uk 2.132.6 and does not register this release, so policyengine.py is installed without its `[uk]` extra and the population runs pass the verified file with `allow_unmanaged=True`. `meta` records the installed model version and the bundle's (`policyengine_bundle_model_version`). When a policyengine.py release bundles this release, the runs switch to the managed dataset.

## Weighting and outputs

- Every weighted total, mean, share and decile uses **microdf**: simulation outputs stay as weighted `MicroSeries` / `MicroDataFrame` objects, and person-to-family and family-to-household mappings use the model's own entity mapping (`map_to`). No weights are applied by hand.
- Record-level outputs stay in the gitignored `.cache/runs`. Each cached run carries a provenance key (dataset revision and sha256, policyengine.py and policyengine-uk versions, and a hash of this repository's engine and scenario code); a cached run whose key does not match the current one is refused, never reused.
- Only weighted aggregates reach `data/results.json`. A breakdown cell resting on fewer than ten gaining records, or fewer than ten records whose value changes, is suppressed; if that leaves one suppressed cell in a breakdown, a second cell is suppressed with it, and if no second cell can be suppressed the whole breakdown is withheld. Suppressed cells are published as `null` with `"suppressed": true`, never as zero.
- Nothing is defaulted: a missing provenance field, an unknown git revision or an empty cell stops the build or is published as suppressed.

## The reform in the model

Two parameters are set to infinity for each fiscal year 2026-27 to 2029-30 and nothing else changes (a test walks the whole parameter tree to check):

| Parameter | How the model applies it |
|---|---|
| `gov.dfe.extended_childcare_entitlement.income.limit` | `extended_childcare_entitlement_meets_income_requirements`: the claimant and partner must each have adjusted net income `<= limit` and quarterly earnings of at least 16 hours at the minimum wage, unless one is on a specified benefit and the other meets those conditions (reg 14(4)/15(4)). The family must be in England and meet the work condition. |
| `gov.hmrc.tax_free_childcare.income.income_limit` | `tax_free_childcare_meets_income_requirements`: the claimant and partner must each have `adjusted_net_income <= limit` (and expected earnings over 13 weeks above 16 hours at the minimum wage). |

Costs are the change in weighted spending:

- **30 hours** = change in `extended_childcare_entitlement` + `universal_childcare_entitlement` + `targeted_childcare_entitlement`. policyengine-uk 2.134.4 keeps the universal and targeted 15 hours when a family becomes eligible for the extended entitlement and counts as extended only each child's funded hours above them (`extended_childcare_entitlement_per_child`). The universal and targeted changes are therefore zero on this release, and all three are summed so nothing is missed.
- **Tax-Free Childcare** = change in `tax_free_childcare` (20% of the family's childcare spending, capped at £2,000 a child, £4,000 if disabled).
- **Net** = minus the change in `gov_balance`. It equals gross (unrounded, to the pound, in every year): no other tax or benefit in the model depends on these limits. Rounded to £m, the published net and gross figures can differ by £1m.

## Years

Each fiscal year 2026-27 to 2029-30 is costed in full, because the proposal gives no start date. 2026-27 is already half over, so the dashboard leads with **2027-28**, the first full year a change could apply; 2026-27 stays in the tables as illustrative.

## Where the model and the law differ

|---|---|---|---|

| 9 | **Expected, not realised, income.** The law tests the adjusted net income a parent *expects* for the tax year when applying or reconfirming (SI 2022/1134 reg 14(3)(c)(i) and 15(3)(b)(i); SI 2015/448 reg 15(1)); the model tests realised annual income. A parent who expected to stay under £100,000 but ended the year above it can already receive support, which the model counts as a reform cost; a parent who expected to exceed £100,000 but ended below it is modelled as eligible today. ## Model corrections

`src/childcare_100k/corrections.py` replaces three policyengine-uk 2.134.4 formulas in every run, baseline and reform alike, and in the household calculator. Each replaced formula is fingerprinted, so a change upstream stops the build instead of being silently overwritten; the engine checks the corrections reached each population simulation, and the household calculator checks they reached its own.

1. **Salary sacrifice returned to pay** from April 2029 is not income for the limits (C4, below): `extended_childcare_entitlement_meets_income_requirements` and `tax_free_childcare_meets_income_requirements`.
2. **Tax-Free Childcare's final income gate tests the claimant and partner** (C6, Tax-Free Childcare half): Childcare Payments Act 2014 s.3(1) requires the claimant's partner to meet the income condition (SI 2015/448 reg 15). 2.134.4's `tax_free_childcare_eligible` still applies it to members flagged `is_parent`, so an unflagged partner (a step-parent, say) over £100,000 passed; the replacement uses upstream's `is_claimant_or_partner`.

Five corrections from earlier versions were removed because policyengine-uk 2.134.4 now does the same (checked against its source, and kept as regression tests in `tests/test_corrections.py`): exactly £100,000 qualifies for the 30 hours (C3: `ani <= p.income.limit`); a partner on a specified benefit need not meet the work and income conditions (C1: `extended_childcare_entitlement_limited_capability_or_specified_benefit` in the work condition); the universal and targeted hours are kept and the extended value is the hours above them (C2: `universal_childcare_entitlement_eligible`, `targeted_childcare_entitlement_eligible`, `extended_childcare_entitlement_per_child`); Tax-Free Childcare regards a partner on a caring or incapacity benefit as in paid work (C5: `tax_free_childcare_regarded_as_in_paid_work`); and the 30 hours' and Tax-Free Childcare's work conditions test the claimant and partner (the rest of C6: `is_claimant_or_partner`).

On this release the two remaining corrections lower the 2029-30 static cost from £1.17bn to £1.124bn on the 30 hours and from £0.259bn to £0.235bn on Tax-Free Childcare, and leave 2027-28 unchanged at the published rounding (£0.964bn and £0.206bn); so the change is in effect the salary sacrifice correction, which applies only from 2029-30.

### Salary sacrifice above the 2029 National Insurance cap

From 6 April 2029, pension contributions made through salary sacrifice above £2,000 a year are subject to Class 1 National Insurance. HMRC's [policy paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/salary-sacrifice-reform-for-pension-contributions-effective-from-6-april-2029/salary-sacrifice-reform-for-pension-contributions) says the measure changes National Insurance only and leaves salary sacrifice's effect on adjusted net income unchanged, naming Tax-Free Childcare among the schemes unaffected.

policyengine-uk 2.134.4 models the cap by adding the excess (`salary_sacrifice_returned_to_income`) to `employment_income`, so it also raises `adjusted_net_income`: a parent on £97,000 after £10,000 of sacrifice reads over £100,000 in 2029-30 and fails both childcare income tests.

The correction applies in every run, baseline and reform alike:
- **What changes:** both income tests compare adjusted net income *less* the returned salary sacrifice with £100,000.
- **What doesn't:** `adjusted_net_income`, income tax and National Insurance are left as the model computes them. Correcting ANI itself would deduct the amount twice, because the model also gives it as pension relief.
- **Before 2029-30:** the correction does nothing.

## Behaviour

The headline costs are **static**: nobody changes how much they work. A labour supply response is costed alongside them (`labour_supply` in the results; `src/childcare_100k/labour_supply.py` and `hours_response.py`, ported from [PolicyEngine/free-childcare-reform](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/free-childcare-reform)) and gives a **dynamic cost** = static total − the money it brings back. The dynamic cost is an illustrative scenario, not a forecast: the hours price elasticity is extrapolated, neither the OBR elasticities nor Brewer's estimate is measured on parents over £100,000, and the range is not a confidence interval. It runs as its own cached job (`labour_supply`) on the central baseline and reform simulations, with the same provenance key as every other run, because the gain to work needs live simulations.

**Who responds.** Every adult (the first two in each family; not self-employed, students or aged 60 and over, the exclusions of both OBR models, Tables A3 and A4) in a family whose **youngest child is under 12** and in which **at least one adult's income, as the limits test it** (adjusted net income less salary sacrifice returned to pay), **is over £100,000** in the baseline: the parent over the limit as well as their partner. Under 12 because Tax-Free Childcare runs to 11 and contains the 30 hours' 9 months-4 years band; following Brewer et al., it is the parent whose *youngest* child is in the band that the support frees to work. Over £100,000 because that is the only place the reform changes anything. Those not in work may move into work (in practice the partner of a parent over the limit, who today gains no childcare support by working and under the reform brings the family the 30 hours and Tax-Free Childcare by doing so); those in work may change their hours. Disabled adults (the model's `is_disabled_for_benefits`, receipt of DLA or PIP) cannot move into work, because the OBR's participation model excludes them (Table A4), but keep the hours response, because its progression model does not (Table A3). The job checks that the reform leaves everyone else's gain to work unchanged. On this release it does not for about 100 adults in 2026-27 and 2027-28 (non-working adults whose own income is just under £100,000, so entering work would take them over the limit). They are not given a response, the effect on the cost is negligible, and `test_labour_supply_block` fails on it until the population definition is settled. About 1.0-1.1 million adults are in the participation population.

**Moving into work (extensive margin).** OBR participation elasticities (Table A1: by sex, partner's work, age of youngest child and earnings quintile; policyengine-uk's `calculate_participation_elasticities`). Each is the percentage change in the probability of working for a percentage change in income in work; Adam and Phillips (IFS R75, Appendix E) convert it to the gain to work G by G / I, where I is in-work income (with G = I − O this is the familiar 1 − replacement rate). Our gain to work nets off childcare, so the factor is the childcare-adjusted G / I actually used. Because the elasticity is a change in the *probability* of working, it applies to the employed share: for a group with employment rate P the employed share rises by P × e × dG/G. As in Adam and Phillips, who reweight the working records, new employment is the weighted sum over responding *workers* of their own e × dG/G; those entrants are given the earnings and fiscal profile of responding non-workers *like the workers who imply them*: the non-workers in the same OBR cell (sex, in a couple or not, the youngest child's age band 0-2, 3-5, 6-10 or 11, and earnings quintile, the groups of Table A1), shared in proportion to each one's own e × dG/G. Where a cell has no responding non-worker, its entrants go to the nearest coarser cell (dropping the quintile, then the child's age band, then everyone); `allocated_by_cell_level` reports how many land at each level. No non-worker's probability exceeds 1; entry above that is dropped and reported (`entry_capped`). A worker whose gain to work falls leaves with probability e × |dG/G|. The gain to work is in-work less out-of-work household net income, each recomputed by the model, **net of the childcare a parent pays to work**: a non-worker is given the mean childcare spend of working parents whose youngest child is the same age, pro-rated to 18.8 hours a week, less the Tax-Free Childcare the scenario would pay on it (from the model's own in-work eligibility); the 30 hours reach them through the model. Entrants work 18.8 hours at the median hourly wage of workers of their sex and age band (policyengine-uk's `impute_wages_for_nonworkers`). Responses are expected values, not random draws. The Exchequer gains an entrant's earnings less the rise in their household's net income (tax and National Insurance paid, less the childcare support the family now receives) and less the Tax-Free Childcare top-up on their new childcare.

**Hours (intensive margin).** Every responding adult in work, at or below £100,000 and over it (published separately), in two parts. The combined earnings change of every responding adult, both parts, is put back into the reform simulation once, which recomputes tax and benefits; the offset is the extra earnings less the rise in household net income. Taxes are not additive in the parts: a parent at £99,900 whose price effect adds £839 and income effect removes £160 crosses the personal-allowance taper, and recomputing the parts separately and adding them overstated that parent's offset by £32 (£432.87 against £400.84, with the income effect on the government-cost basis below). The parts are therefore an attribution that adds up to the net exactly: the adults at or below £100,000 recomputed on their own and those over it as the remainder; within each group, the price change recomputed on its own and the income effect as the remainder. Hours and earnings move together at a constant hourly wage.

- *Price effect* (`hours_response.py`): an assumed childcare-price elasticity of hours of −0.042 times the change in the price of the family's **marginal** hour of paid childcare, for adults whose family pays for childcare. The elasticity is an extrapolated scenario assumption, not an estimated price elasticity: Brewer, Cattan, Crawford and Rabe (IFS WP20/09, Table A.3 panel B) estimate +0.600 weekly hours, on a mean of 14.319 including zeros for non-workers, for mothers whose youngest child becomes eligible for full-time instead of part-time free care. Treating that eligibility as a 100% price fall extends it to other interventions, to fathers, to child ages and to incomes it was not estimated on; it is a total-hours estimate, so it overlaps with the extensive margin. **What counts as a price change**: only a change in what an extra hour of care costs. The marginal net price of the next £1 of paid care is £1 less all the cash support the model ties to `childcare_expenses` on it (Tax-Free Childcare, the Universal Credit childcare element, and the legacy tax-credit, Housing Benefit and Childcare Grant links): the model's own rate, the change in that support when the family buys £1 more care (split across its children in proportion to their spend, so near a cap it is the rate on the next pound, not an average over spending beyond it), today and, under the reform, at the spend left once the funded hours displace paid care, the income effect's spending. Tax-Free Childcare pays 20% of each routed pound until the child's cap (with the 59.3% routed share, about 12p per £1), so where the reform newly pays it below the cap it lowers the price (a family at the £2,000 cap on £11,000 of care faces 20% on the £7,710.38 left); the UC childcare element pays 85p per £1 up to its cap (SI 2013/376 regs 34 and 36), so where displacement takes a UC family below the cap its next £1 falls from £1 to 15p (María's A8 household: £27,000 of care, element capped at £22,033.92, £20,153.82 on the £23,710.38 left). Sensitivities (`intensive_price_basis`): the reform's rate at today's spend (`original_spend`, hours offset £0.158bn in 2029-30) and Tax-Free Childcare alone (`tfc_only`, the rule before A8, £0.168bn) against £0.163bn central. The 30 funded hours are a fixed amount, conditional only on both parents meeting the minimum earnings test, so for a family that still buys paid care on top of them they do not change what an extra hour costs: they are inframarginal and enter only through the income effect. Only where they are worth more than all the paid care the family buys does the marginal hour become free (a 100% fall, Brewer's setting); funded hours worth exactly the spend cover the hours bought, and the next hour is still paid for. Spending is recorded in pounds, not hours, so coverage is judged on value: newly funded hours are *assumed* to displace paid care at 90.5% of their value (IFS BN189, pp. 11-12: 570 more funded hours raised subsidisable care by 163 hours and all non-family care by 54; 1 − 54/570 counts every displaced hour of non-family care as paid; 1 − 163/570 = 71.4% is the lower end, 100% the upper). The rule is the same for both groups, since a family buys one set of childcare.
- *Income effect*: the OBR income elasticities (Table A2; policyengine-uk's `calculate_labour_net_income_elasticities`: −0.185 for a woman in a couple whose youngest child is 0-2, down to −0.037 for a lone mother of a 0-4-year-old; −0.05 for a man in a couple), applied as policyengine-uk's `apply_progression_responses` does: earnings × elasticity × the household's gain from the reform, before any response, as a percentage of its disposable income (`hbai_household_net_income`; clipped to ±100%). Every responding adult in work. A family made better off works slightly less, so this part costs money. The OBR elasticities are responses to cash income, and the funded hours are not cash: what they put in a family's pocket is the paid childcare they replace. So the gain is measured on the same disposable-income basis as its denominator: the change in `hbai_household_net_income` (cash taxes and benefits, Tax-Free Childcare included, funded hours not) plus the paid care the newly funded hours displace (their funding value at the 90.5% displacement assumption below, capped at what the family pays for care, summed over the household's families). The reform's cash income is recomputed with each child's spend lowered by the family's displaced share, so the Tax-Free Childcare top-up and Universal Credit childcare element on the care it stops buying go together (a London UC family on £100,001 and £16,000 loses £2,796.18 of childcare element; in the population this moves the offset by under £100). For a 2027-28 couple on £120,000 and £40,000 with a three-year-old in £5,000 of paid care, the newly funded hours displace £3,289.62 of it; the reform's top-up on the £1,710.38 left is £342.08, not £1,000, so the gain is £3,631.70 rather than £4,289.62. Two sensitivities (`intensive_income_basis`): holding childcare spending fixed (row 6), so that support on displaced care is kept (`paid_care_fixed_spend`), and counting the funded hours at what the government pays for them (`government_cost`, the change in `household_net_income`). The base is disposable income rather than `household_net_income`, which also deducts policyengine-uk's expected stamp duty: one surveyed family (weight about 8,000) with £160,000 of earnings and £106,000 a year of expected stamp duty had a household net income falling from £9,600 in 2026-27 to £1,400 in 2028-29 and below zero in 2029-30, so its percentage change, and the over-£100,000 income effect, jumped in 2028-29 and fell back.

**Couples.** The OBR groups in Tables A1 and A2 ("married or cohabiting" women, lone parents, men other than lone fathers) turn on whether a person has a partner. policyengine-uk 2.134.4 still assigns them with `is_married`, which Microcosm supplies as legal marriage or civil partnership, so a cohabiting father would get no elasticity and a cohabiting mother the lone-parent rates; about a fifth of couples with children are not married. The participation and income elasticities here are assigned to married and cohabiting couples alike, by the model's `is_couple` (`labour_supply.CoupleView`), leaving `is_married` and the tax that depends on it untouched ([policyengine-uk#2190](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-uk/issues/2190)).

The displacement rate also sets how much paid care the funded hours save in the income effect: the `intensive_displacement` sensitivity moves the 2029-30 hours offset from £0.163bn to £0.149bn at 71.4% and £0.184bn at 100%. `tfc_only` differs slightly from the central figure, so on this release the Universal Credit childcare element moves some paying adults' marginal price. In the income effect (`intensive_income_basis`), keeping the support on displaced care makes it −£0.057bn rather than −£0.053bn and the hours offset £0.159bn; counting the funded hours at government cost makes it −£0.091bn and the hours offset £0.124bn. (The record counts behind these, such as how many paying adults the funded hours fully cover, were last measured on the 2 October release and are not restated.)

**Range.** Low and high scale every elasticity (participation, price and income) by 1/3 and 2. The range is illustrative, not a sourced uncertainty interval: the factors are the ratios of childcare-price elasticities of maternal *employment* of −0.05 and −0.30 to a central −0.15 (a reading of Akgündüz and Plantenga's meta-analysis for the UK, whose Table 1 gives UK responses of −0.09 for single and −0.138 for married mothers), a different outcome from the OBR participation and income elasticities and the hours price elasticity they scale. The dynamic range below should not be read as a confidence interval.

**Why so few move into work.** In 2029-30 about 1.14 million adults respond. The OBR elasticities with respect to in-work income fall sharply with respect to the gain to work, because the gain to work is small next to the household's in-work income for a partner whose partner earns over £100,000. Applied to the employed share, that gives about 2,300 entrants in 2029-30. About 800 are placed among non-workers in the same OBR cell as the workers who imply them and about 1,500 in the same sex, couple and child-age group without the earnings quintile; none needs a coarser cell, and the cap of 1 on a non-worker's probability drops none. `entry_sensitivity` separates the effect of the coarser-cell entrants, at central elasticities: dropping them leaves about 800 entrants and an offset of −£7.5m (`same_cell_only`), and giving them the weighted median hourly wage of the workers who imply them turns the offset to −£11.2m (`worker_profile`), against −£18.5m centrally. They cost money rather than bring it back: the tax and National Insurance on 18.8 hours is outweighed by the 30 hours and Tax-Free Childcare their family now receives, so the extensive offset is −£0.019bn. There are no leavers.

**Not modelled: bunching.** Parents who today keep their income at or just below £100,000 and would earn more without the limit do not appear in the survey data, so their response is not modelled. CenTax estimate it from HMRC records at +£210m in 2029-30 (free hours only).

**Against CenTax.** CenTax (Table 4.2, 2030 = 2029-30, free hours only) find +£130m from partners entering work and +£210m from parents no longer keeping income below £100,000. Our extensive margin covers both schemes and brings back −£0.019bn in 2029-30: CenTax's partner response implies far more entrants than the OBR's elasticities give for second earners in high-income households, and it is not clear that CenTax net off the childcare support entrants' families then receive. Their £210m has no counterpart here, and our hours response has none in theirs. Neither figure is a target.

The household calculator and the breakdowns by income, family type and region are static.

## Take-up

Take-up holds Microcosm's existing `would_claim_extended_childcare` and `would_claim_tfc` draws fixed; it is not modelled afresh for newly eligible families. The draw rates differ by income; `assumptions` in the results gives each rate overall and where a parent is over £100,000. A Tax-Free Childcare top-up also needs recorded childcare spending.

## Baseline validation

`baseline_validation` sets the unreformed model against HMRC's income tax liabilities statistics (people with income of £100,000 or more, July 2026 Table 2.5), HMRC's Tax-Free Childcare statistics (June 2026), DfE's January 2026 early years census and DfE's early years funding technical notes for 2025-26 and 2026-27 (free-entitlements funding: each total is the sum of the note's illustrative totals by age, since neither note states a combined figure). The figures are in the README's validation table and `baseline_validation`. In short: the high-earner count and Tax-Free Childcare match HMRC closely; the shipped baseline funds about 350,000 4-year-olds the data record in reception, which DfE does not count, so its 3- and 4-year-olds on the working-parent entitlement and its 2026-27 funded-hours spending are above DfE's; without reception the 3- and 4-year-olds match DfE (379,000 against 388,700), and spending on CenTax's basis is within 2% of DfE's 2026-27 total. Under-3s are below DfE's count, which includes about 31,000 children aged 9-11 months the model cannot hold.

## The £0.7bn benchmark

The Conservatives put the cost at about £700m a year when they announced the pledge (PA: "The Conservatives estimate the policy would cost £700 million a year"; BBC), funded by cutting staff at arm's-length public bodies (about £1.6bn by the end of the decade). The party's web announcement covers both the free hours and Tax-Free Childcare ("abolish the £100,000 childcare cliff edge for both free childcare hours and Tax-Free Childcare") and gives no figure or method. City AM says the costing "is based on a recent report by the Centre for the Analysis of Taxation" and that removing the Tax-Free Childcare limit as well "pushed up the costs slightly". CenTax label tax years by their later year (report p.2), so their "2030" is 2029-30, the year we compare. CenTax's report ([*Removing the childcare cliff-edge: impacts and cost of reform*](https://centax.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/AdvaniFlewPepin-HallSummers2026_Removing-the-childcare-cliff-edge.pdf), September 2026, Table 4.2) covers the **free childcare hours only**: a **static** cost of £980m in 2030, and a **net** cost of £640m after £340m of extra revenue: £210m of tax from parents who no longer keep their income below £100,000 (the intensive margin) and £130m of tax and National Insurance from partners who enter work (the extensive margin; Table 4.2 and the sentence after it). It does not cover Tax-Free Childcare.

The like-for-like comparison is therefore **our 30-hours leg against CenTax's static figures** (both static, both the free hours only): £800m in 2028 (2027-28) and £980m in 2030 (2029-30), or £520m and £640m net. As shipped, our 30-hours leg is £0.964bn in 2027-28 and £1.124bn in 2029-30, 21% and 15% above CenTax's static figures. On CenTax's basis (no funded hours in reception, DfE's funding rates, adjusted net income after pension contributions, and 9-11-month-olds; section above) it is £0.872bn and £0.961bn, against CenTax's £0.80bn and £0.98bn after their deduction for parents bunching just under £100,000 and £0.86bn and £1.07bn before it. A bootstrap over FRS source households by the Microcosm team ([#8](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-childcare-100k-threshold/pull/8#issuecomment-6327525773)) gives a standard deviation of about £0.13bn in 2027-28 and £0.18bn in 2029-30 on the shipped leg; CenTax's figures are within one standard deviation of ours on their basis. The cost rests on about 2,000 gaining survey records (`reconciliation.concentration`: a Kish effective sample size of about 175, the ten largest carrying 27% in 2027-28), against CenTax's HMRC records for the whole population. The remaining differences are not decomposed: take-up, the income distribution above £100,000, and expected against realised income differ. Microcosm has about 300,000 adults over £100,000 with a child under 5 in 2027-28 (264,000 on income after pension contributions; `reconciliation.parents_over_100k_with_child_under_5_thousands`), against CenTax's roughly 200,000 parents of a child aged 9 months to 4; by the Microcosm team's reading (#8), CenTax can only count parents they find in HMRC records, which misses many single-earner couples, and those mostly fail the partner work test. The £0.7bn is close to CenTax's net free-hours figure, £0.64bn, plus a small Tax-Free Childcare addition. Our total covers both schemes on the same static basis as our 30-hours leg.

## Cliff example

One illustrative family, computed with policyengine.py's `calculate_household()` for 2027-28: a couple in the South East with children aged 2 and 3, £20,000 a year of their own childcare spending, one parent on £40,000. Household net income after that spending is £101,619 when the other parent earns £99,000, £102,199 at exactly £100,000 (which still qualifies, as in law), and £84,332 at £100,001; with the limits removed it is £102,200 at £100,001, a cliff of about £17,900.
